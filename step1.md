Step 1 - Secure the Link

Part 1: BB84 and Eavesdropper Detection
built a BB84 simulation to show how two people can create a shared secret key and check whether someone may have intercepted the qubits. Alice generates random bits and randomly chooses a basis for each one. She encodes each bit as a qubit and sends it to Bob. Bob independently chooses a random basis to measure each qubit. Afterward, Alice and Bob compare their basis choices over a public channel and keep only the bits where they used the same basis. These remaining bits are called the sifted key. The team tested an intercept-and-resend eavesdropper. Eve measures each qubit in a randomly chosen basis, then prepares a new qubit based on her result and sends it to Bob. If Eve chooses the wrong basis, her measurement can change the state. Alice and Bob estimate the quantum bit error rate (QBER) by comparing a sample of their sifted bits.
Part 2: Results| with no eavesdropper, the local simulator produced a QBER of 0.0. With Eve intercepting and resending the qubits, the QBER was 0.237, or about 24%. This is close to the expected error rate of about 25% for this attack: Eve chooses the wrong basis about half the time, and those wrong-basis measurements cause errors in about half of the affected sifted bits. The team also tested a batched circuit with 16 qubits and 200 shots. The local simulator gave a QBER of 0.0. The same type of test was submitted to the IonQ simulator through qBraid and also gave a QBER of 0.0 for that run. This was a simulator result, not a run on physical quantum hardware.
Part 3: What the Result Shows|The comparison shows how an intercept-and-resend attack can raise the error rate in the sifted key. A higher QBER can warn Alice and Bob that the communication may have been disturbed. It does not identify who caused the errors: noise, transmission problems, or device errors could also raise QBER. A QBER of 0.0 in one run does not prove that nobody intercepted the qubits; it only means that the tested bits showed no errors in that run.
Part 4: Limits and Use in the Grid|This is a small demonstration of BB84, not a complete key distribution system. In a real deployment, Alice and Bob would need to authenticate their public discussion, compare only a sample of the sifted key, and discard the tested bits. They would also need error correction and privacy amplification before using the remaining bits as a key. BB84 can help protect a key exchange on a suitable quantum link, but it does not encrypt grid traffic by itself or secure every substation and control center. The resulting key would still need to be used by ordinary encryption equipment. The circuit tests also do not demonstrate a deployed fiber link or prove that QKD is the most cost-effective choice for a utility. For a small or resource-limited grid, the cost and operational requirements of a QKD link would need to be compared with post-quantum cryptography
